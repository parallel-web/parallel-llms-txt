# Company research

Company research is how you learn what a business does, how it makes money, who runs it, and where it is heading before you interview there, sell to it, invest in it, or buy it. This guide covers a seven-step method, the free and paid sources worth checking, checklists for four kinds of researchers, and how to automate the work with AI agents and APIs.

Company research is the process of gathering and analyzing information about a business, including its operations, finances, leadership, market, and competitors, to make an informed decision.


## What is company research?

Company research means collecting and making sense of public and private information about a company to answer a specific question. That question might be whether to sell to it, invest in it, work for it, or compete with it. You start with the decision, then gather the facts it needs.

Sales teams, investors, job seekers, recruiters, and partnership leads all run this work. Each person asks a different question, so each one weighs different facts about the same business.

The work turns scattered facts into a profile you can act on. Those facts include firmographics, financials, news, hiring signals, and the company’s tech stack.

On their own they read as noise. Pulled together, they show who a company is and where it’s headed.

Most research starts with [AI web search](https://parallel.ai/products/search) across authoritative web sources, then narrows to the details that answer your question.

## How to research a company in seven steps

The same method works for an interview on Thursday or a deal memo due next month; only the depth changes.

1. **Write down the decision.** "Should I take this job?" and "Is this account worth a quarter of outreach?" need different facts. The question tells you what to collect and when to stop.
2. **Pin down the exact company.** Record the legal name, website domain, headquarters, and whether it is public or private. A ticker or SEC Central Index Key (CIK) identifies a U.S. public company; a company number identifies a UK one. Similar names and subsidiaries are easy to confuse ([entity matching](https://parallel.ai/learn/entity-matching) covers why).
3. **Read what the company says about itself.** The website, careers page, press releases, and investor relations section show what it sells, to whom, and how it describes its strategy.
4. **Check official records.** For U.S. public companies, [SEC EDGAR](https://www.sec.gov/search-filings) is free: the 10-K annual report covers the business, risk factors, and audited financials, the 10-Q updates them quarterly, and an 8-K discloses material events between reports. UK companies file accounts, officers, and persons with significant control at [Companies House](https://find-and-update.company-information.service.gov.uk/).
5. **Look at the people.** Leadership bios, LinkedIn profiles, and open roles show who makes decisions, how the team is organized, and which functions are growing.
6. **Collect outside views.** Read the last 12 months of news, then customer reviews (G2, Trustpilot, app stores) and employee reviews (Glassdoor, Indeed). List the two or three closest competitors and how the company compares.
7. **Write a one-page profile with sources.** Put each fact next to its link and the date you checked it, and note what you could not confirm.

## Where to find company information

Public companies disclose far more than private ones, so for a private company expect to lean on news, funding announcements, and registries.

| Source | What it gives you | Best for | Cost |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Company website and investor relations | Products, leadership, press releases, annual reports | Everyone | Free |
| SEC EDGAR | 10-K, 10-Q, 8-K, proxy statements, and Form D notices for U.S. companies | Investors, M&A, candidates at public companies | Free |
| Companies House (UK) | Accounts, officers, persons with significant control, filing history | Due diligence, KYB, UK suppliers | Free |
| LinkedIn | Headcount, leadership, team structure, recent hires | Sales, recruiting, job seekers | Free, with paid tiers |
| Crunchbase | Funding rounds, investors, acquisitions | Sales, startup investors | Free tier, with paid plans |
| News and press releases | Launches, layoffs, leadership changes, deals | Everyone | Mostly free |
| Glassdoor and Indeed | Culture, interview process, pay ranges | Job seekers | Free |
| G2, Trustpilot, app stores | Customer sentiment and product complaints | Sales, investors | Free |
| Licensed databases (PitchBook, S&P Capital IQ, D&B Hoovers) | Private-company financials, ownership, comparables | Investors, M&A | Paid subscription |

Private companies still leave a trail. A U.S. company raising money under Regulation D [must file a Form D](https://www.sec.gov/resources-small-businesses/exempt-offerings/filing-form-d-notice) within 15 days after the first sale, and those notices are public on EDGAR, so you can confirm that a round happened and roughly when.

## Key characteristics

Good research shares a few traits, whatever the decision behind it.

- **Question driven:** Every effort starts with a decision it needs to support.
- **Multiple sources:** It pulls from public filings, company websites, news, review sites, and directories.
- **Freshness matters:** Demand for fresh, structured company data keeps rising, and [data enrichment market growth](https://www.thebusinessresearchcompany.com/report/data-enrichment-solutions-global-market-report) is projected to reach $5.13 billion by 2030 at a 12.2% CAGR, according to The Business Research Company (2026).
- **Accuracy has a cost:** Gartner research from 2020 found the [cost of poor data quality](https://www.gartner.com/en/data-analytics/topics/data-quality) reaches at least $12.9 million per year on average.

## Company research checklist by use case

Use the list that matches your decision.

### Job seekers

- What the company sells, to whom, and how it makes money
- News from the last year: funding, layoffs, mergers, or leadership changes
- The team you would join, your likely manager, and how long people stay
- Employee reviews on Glassdoor or Indeed, read for recurring themes
- For a public company, the business and risk factors sections of the latest 10-K

### Sales teams

- Firmographics: industry, headcount, locations, and revenue range
- Trigger events such as a new funding round, a new executive, or hiring for the function you sell to ([how to monitor sales trigger events](https://parallel.ai/articles/how-to-monitor-sales-trigger-events-that-actually-convert))
- Current vendors and tech stack
- The people likely to sit on the buying committee

### Investors

- Business model, revenue drivers, and margins over several periods
- Management track record, ownership, and insider holdings
- Competitive position and market share
- Risks disclosed in filings, plus open litigation

### M&A and due diligence

- Legal entity structure, subsidiaries, and beneficial owners ([KYB](https://parallel.ai/learn/kyb) covers the verification side)
- Litigation, regulatory actions, sanctions, and adverse media
- Customer concentration and key contracts
- Intellectual property, licenses, and any change-of-control clauses

For deal teams that run this checklist on many targets, [AI due diligence](https://parallel.ai/articles/how-to-build-an-automated-due-diligence-research-pipeline) walks through an automated pipeline with citations.

- **Verifiable:** Good research traces each fact to its source, which supports efforts to [discover companies at scale](https://parallel.ai/products/findall) that match specific criteria.

## Example

Picture a sales rep researching a target account before outreach. They pull the founding year, headcount, funding history, and recent news into one briefing.

An investor runs the same play before a first meeting. They add ownership structure, revenue signals, and competitor moves, so the conversation starts from evidence.

The time cost is real. The [Salesforce State of Sales report](https://www.salesforce.com/news/stories/state-of-sales-report-announcement-2026/) found that the average seller spends 40% of their week selling. Tasks like data entry and internal meetings consume the rest.

Automated research changes that math. Modern tools search, read, and synthesize sources into a structured, cited company profile in minutes. You can [run deep research](https://parallel.ai/products/task) without stitching the pieces together by hand.

## FAQ

**What are the main sources for company research?**

The main sources are public filings such as SEC EDGAR for U.S. public companies, the company’s own website, news coverage, review sites, and business directories.

**How is company research different from market research?**

It focuses on one specific business. Market research studies a whole market, industry, or customer segment.

**Can company research be automated?**

Yes, AI research tools can search the web, read sources, and return structured company profiles with citations in minutes.

**How do you research a private company?**

Combine funding announcements, LinkedIn, and job postings with registry filings: Form D notices on EDGAR in the U.S., and annual accounts and officer lists at Companies House in the UK.

**What should you research about a company before an interview?**

Cover what it sells and to whom, how it makes money, news from the last year, the team you would join, and what employees say about the culture. For a public company, the business and risk factors sections of its latest 10-K give you specific talking points.
