## What original content is and how to create it

Original content adds information, evidence, or perspective that readers can’t get from pages that already rank or from an AI model’s answer. To create it, set a goal, define the reader, and write down your own view before you research. Then map what existing sources say, find the gap, and fill it with evidence only you can provide, verified and cited.

Google uses the same yardstick. In [Google’s people-first content guidance](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content), Google asks, “Does the content provide original information, reporting, research, or analysis?” Google also asks whether content offers “substantial value when compared to other pages in search results.” The same page describes originality as “unique, original information or perspectives that aren’t already available on other websites.”

We call that relative measure _information gain_: what a page adds beyond the sources a reader or a model already has. It’s a working definition for editors, and Google hasn’t said it ranks pages on a metric by that name. Because originality is relative, you have to read what exists before you can judge your own draft.

The process has eight steps:

1. Set the goal and the success metric.
2. Define the reader and the question they need answered.
3. Write your point of view before you research.
4. Map what already ranks and gets cited.
5. Find the gap and choose your angle.
6. Gather evidence only you can provide.
7. Draft, verify, and cite every claim.
8. Publish, adapt, and keep it current.

You build originality into the evidence and the angle. The format carries it to the reader, and a blog post or a podcast episode can deliver the same finding.

## Why original content matters more in AI search

Two shifts raised the bar at the same time. Machines now write a large share of new articles, and AI answer engines summarize the web before a reader clicks.

In both cases, the page that gains is the source an answer can’t do without.

AI agents add a third pressure. In our own Index data, a source’s contribution to agent answers drops when other sites carry the same information. It rises when the source carries something nobody else has.

### AI-generated articles now match human output

In [Graphite’s 2026 study of new articles](https://graphite.io/five-percent/research/ai-now-writes-as-many-online-articles-as-humans-do), Graphite researchers estimated that 49.9% of new English articles in Q1 2026 were primarily generated by AI. That share has plateaued since early 2025, when the split stood at 49.6% AI and 50.4% human. Graphite sampled 55.4k English URLs from Common Crawl and averaged three AI detectors, so treat the numbers as detector estimates.

Ahrefs measured something different. In [Ahrefs’ analysis of 900,000 new pages](https://ahrefs.com/blog/what-percentage-of-new-content-is-ai-generated/), Ahrefs found that 74.2% of new English pages from April 2025 contained some AI content. Only 2.5% were pure AI. Most of those pages mixed human and machine writing, so the 74.2% figure doesn’t mean AI wrote three quarters of the web.

The two studies use different samples and definitions, and we keep them apart. Both point to the same condition for you as a writer. Generic coverage of almost any topic is now cheap and abundant, so assume your reader has seen the summary before reaching your page.

### AI answers send fewer readers to the sources

Google calls its generated summaries _AI Overviews_: answers that appear above the traditional results on the search page. [Pew Research Center’s study of AI summaries](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/07/22/google-users-are-less-likely-to-click-on-links-when-an-ai-summary-appears-in-the-results/) analyzed March 2025 browsing data from 900 US adults. Users clicked a traditional search result in 8% of visits with an AI summary, compared with 15% of visits without one.

About 18% of the searches in Pew’s data produced a summary. The data is observational and covers Google searches in the US over one month, so it shows an association between summaries and fewer clicks.

For you, the implication is about which page earns the citation. A reader who wanted a restated fact gets it on the results page and moves on. The page that supplied a unique fact has the strongest case for the citation. That position is worth more when each search sends fewer clicks.

### AI agents reward sources they can’t replace

We studied the same question from the agent side. In our [Visibility-Value Gap research](https://index.parallel.ai/the-visibility-value-gap), published by Parallel’s research team on July 31, 2026, we ranked domains two ways. _Visibility_ measures how often a domain’s pages surface when AI systems look for answers.

_Value_ uses the _Shapley value_, a method from game theory that splits credit for an outcome by how much each contributor changed it. For a web source, that means its marginal contribution to the final answer. The two rankings overlap less than you’d expect, and domains fall into four groups:

- **Expected winners:** high visibility and high value.
- **Overexposed:** high visibility and low value. Examples include medium.com (value rank 108, gap 57) and scribd.com (value rank 122, gap 60).
- **Underrated:** low visibility and high value. Examples include arxiv.org (value rank 37, gap 98), fda.gov (64, 51), and census.gov (49, 41).
- **Long tail:** low visibility and low value.

Uniqueness explains the gap. If a hundred sites report the same fact, each earns little, even if AI systems cite all hundred often. As we wrote in the report, “Visibility rewards ubiquity, while Shapley value rewards irreplaceability.”

Our report explains why the underrated group ranks high. It describes arxiv.org as “frequently the original location of a specific finding,” so an agent that needs a paper’s actual number has one place to get it. For fda.gov, the report notes that “Only the FDA’s own record of that approval is the fact rather than a report about the fact.”

Our analysis draws on one week of Index activity and skews toward enterprise use cases. Read the rankings as a snapshot of agent behavior on our platform.

Index by Parallel gives content owners this view of their own domain, based on how Parallel’s agents use it. Traditional analytics count visits, and Index shows use inside answers. You can read [how Index measures contribution](https://parallel.ai/blog/introducing-index-by-parallel): you see which queries your content answers, how often agents reference it, and how unique its contribution is.

Google points the same way for its own AI features. [Google’s guide to generative AI features](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/ai-optimization-guide) advises publishers to offer a unique point of view. In the same guide, Google writes, “Don’t just recycle what others on the internet have already said, or could easily be produced by a generative AI model.”

## Original, derivative, and commodity content

You can sort any page by how easy it is to replace. This classification follows the logic of the Visibility-Value Gap, where the sources that are hardest to substitute carry the most weight in an answer.

| Type | What it contains | Example | How easy it is to replace |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Commodity content | Facts available on many pages | A definition of content marketing or a list of common writing tips | Easy: a similar page or an AI answer covers it |
| Derivative content | A synthesis of others’ work with light added framing | A roundup of published studies with short commentary | Moderate: useful, but another synthesis can stand in |
| Original content | Something new, such as data you collected or analysis you did | A customer survey published with its method and sample | Hard: any answer that uses the finding needs the source |

Original content comes from five sources:

- **First-party data:** product usage or customer outcomes you measured yourself.
- **First-hand experience:** what your team learned running a launch or a migration, including what failed.
- **Expert analysis:** a practitioner’s judgment on tradeoffs that a summary can’t supply.
- **Primary-source reporting:** interviews, documents, or records you obtained and read yourself.
- **Original frameworks and tools:** a new method, calculator, or template your reader can apply.

Blogs, videos, podcasts, infographics, and content offers such as reports can all carry original material. Choose the format that fits how your reader consumes information, and keep the finding the same across formats.

## Step-by-step: how to create original content

You do the research first so you have something new to say by the time you draft. Steps 3 through 6 do most of the work.

Steps 1 and 2 keep that research pointed at a real reader with a real question. Steps 7 and 8 keep the published piece accurate.

### 1. Set the goal and the success metric

Decide what the piece must do: build awareness, drive engagement, convert readers, or earn citations in AI answers. Pick the metric before you write, because the goal changes the evidence you need.

A goal of “get cited when people ask about X” means you need a fact or dataset about X that other pages lack. It also means you’ll track citations alongside pageviews. Keep each piece to one primary metric so you can tell whether it worked.

### 2. Define the reader and the question they need answered

Build a lightweight persona: the reader’s role, the problem that brought them to search, what they know, and what would make them bookmark the page. Then list three to five questions they’d type into a search box or ask an AI assistant.

Those questions do double duty. They’re the queries your page has to answer, and they’re close to the prompts AI answer engines receive.

Use real inputs to write them. Sales calls, support tickets, and community threads show you the words readers use, and invented profiles don’t.

### 3. Write your point of view before you research

Before you read a single competing page, write 100 to 200 words on what you believe about the topic. Include what you’ve seen firsthand and where you disagree with common advice.

This order protects your angle. If you read ten guides first, you’ll absorb their consensus and mistake it for your own view.

Mark each claim in your notes as one you can prove now or one that needs evidence. The second list becomes your research plan for step 6.

### 4. Map what already ranks and gets cited

A _consensus map_ is a record of what existing sources say about your topic. To build one, pull the pages that rank for your query and the sources AI answers cite, then read each page in full. Record the claims they repeat, the evidence they cite, and the questions they skip.

For Google AI Overviews, the ranking set is a fair proxy for what the answers draw from. In [Ahrefs’ study of AI Overview citations](https://ahrefs.com/blog/search-rankings-ai-citations/), Ahrefs found that 76.10% of pages cited in AI Overviews ranked in the top 10. The July 2025 study covered 1.9M citations from 1M AI Overviews, and Ahrefs describes the correlation as positive yet moderate.

Reading tab by tab makes it easy to miss which claims repeat and which questions nobody answers. With the [Parallel Search API](https://parallel.ai/products/search), you describe your objective in natural language and get back ranked URLs with dense excerpts. You then pass each URL to the [Extract API](https://parallel.ai/products/extract) and get clean markdown, so you can compare full pages side by side.

We ran this step for this article. On October 12, 2026, we pulled six top English-language guides for “how to create original content” from our Google and AI search analysis and from Parallel Search, then read each one in full with Parallel Extract. The six pages shared four patterns:

- **Google’s guidance:** none of the six linked to Google’s current helpful content guidance.
- **AI source selection:** none explained how AI answer engines choose the sources they cite.
- **Information gain:** none used the term.
- **Novelty checks:** five of six recommended surveys or original research, and none showed how to check whether a finding already exists.

Six pages, one query, and one date make this a small sample, and we don’t generalize it to the category. It does show a concrete pattern: these guides tell you to say something new and give you no method for checking what already exists.

### 5. Find the gap and choose your angle

Put your point of view from step 3 next to the consensus map from step 4. The gaps show up where the two disagree or where the map goes quiet:

- **Missing evidence:** claims that pages repeat with no source behind them.
- **Unanswered questions:** questions from step 2 that no ranking page addresses.
- **Outdated data:** statistics with a newer edition or a changed underlying fact.
- **A supportable contrarian position:** a point where you disagree with consensus and can prove it.
- **An uncovered segment:** a reader group or use case that nobody writes for.

Pick one primary angle and state it in a sentence. If you can’t write that sentence, you don’t have an angle yet.

For new ideas, start with the question map from step 2 and group related questions into topic clusters. Then look for trends you can document with dates. Treat each gap as a hypothesis until you’ve confirmed that nobody else has published the finding.

### 6. Gather evidence only you can provide

Your options include product or customer data, a survey of your audience, or a small experiment. Expert interviews, documented case studies, and a dataset you build from the web also work. Pick the one that answers your angle with the least room for doubt.

Survey data points the same way. [Orbit Media’s 2026 survey of 1,042 marketers](https://www.orbitmedia.com/blog/blogging-statistics/) associates publishing original research with a 50% higher likelihood of reporting strong results. The same survey found that 92.4% of respondents use AI for blogging, and Orbit reports that AI use “correlates with nothing.”

Respondents reported their own results, and the findings are correlational, so read them as a signal about practice and leave causation open. Andy Crestodina, co-founder of Orbit Media, says of original research, “This is something AI cannot do.”

Some studies need a sample from the live web, such as a list of companies in a niche that shipped a specific feature. The [FindAll API](https://parallel.ai/products/findall) builds that dataset from a natural language query. It generates candidates, evaluates each one against your conditions, and enriches the matches with structured fields.

Traditional data providers sell static datasets that go stale, and FindAll generates yours on demand. For research that pulls from many sources, the [Task API](https://parallel.ai/products/task) returns structured findings with citations.

Record the method, sample, and date for anything you publish. Readers can’t check a number without those three details.

### 7. Draft, verify, and cite every claim

Write to your angle. Lead each section with the claim, then give the evidence.

[Google’s guidance on generative AI content](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/using-gen-ai-content) says AI “can be particularly useful when researching a topic, and to add structure to original content.” Google also says, “It is critical to manually factcheck and review all AI-generated content for accuracy and trustworthiness before publishing.”

Verify each statistic at its original publisher, and note the sample, date, and scope. Write “is associated with” for correlations. Link each claim to its source and name the source in the same sentence.

If you run research through the Task API, the [Basis framework](https://parallel.ai/blog/introducing-basis-with-calibrated-confidences) attaches citations, reasoning, and a calibrated confidence score to each output field. You can route claims with low confidence to a human editor first. Your editors then spend their time where the evidence is thinnest.

### 8. Publish, adapt, and keep it current

Publish with clear headings, a direct answer at the top, tables for comparisons, and dated sources. Readers can scan that structure, and AI systems can extract the finding from it.

Adapt the core finding for each channel instead of reposting the full article. A chart works on LinkedIn, a short clip works on video platforms, and a thread can walk through the findings one at a time.

Put each piece on an editorial calendar tied to the goal from step 1, and schedule a date to revisit its data. You can set up the [Monitor API](https://parallel.ai/products/monitor) to track the web for new developments on your topic. It sends a webhook alert when something new appears, so you know when a statistic you published has changed.

## How to measure whether your content is original and working

Traffic tells you whether people visited. It doesn’t tell you whether AI answers cited your page or whether agents relied on it. Track three layers:

| Layer | What to track | What it tells you |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Search and site | Organic traffic, rankings, time on page, conversions | Whether people find the page and act on it |
| AI search | Citations of your page in AI Overviews and answer engines | Whether AI answers use your page as a source |
| Agent usage | Impressions, citations, value, and uniqueness in Index by Parallel | How Parallel’s agents use your content and how hard it is to replace |

On AI Overview queries, cited pages earn more clicks than uncited ones. [Seer Interactive’s 2026 AI Overview analysis](https://www.seerinteractive.com/insights/aio-impact-on-google-ctr-2026-update) covered 53 brands between January 2025 and February 2026. Seer found that cited pages earned about 120% more organic clicks per impression than uncited pages on AI Overview queries. On informational queries, Seer found that cited pages still earned 38% fewer clicks per impression than results on queries with no AI Overview.

Seer states, “We cannot claim causation,” and notes that brands with more authority are also more likely to earn citations. Treat the finding as a reason to track citations, and keep the claim at association.

For agent usage, [check your domain on Index](https://index.parallel.ai/). Index by Parallel reports four scores per domain: impressions, citations, value, and uniqueness. The uniqueness score measures “How hard your content is to replace,” so you can see whether your pages read as differentiated work or commodity coverage.

## Common mistakes and how to avoid them

These five mistakes show up in research and verification:

- **Paraphrasing a paraphrase:** trace each fact to its original publisher, the way arxiv.org and fda.gov rank as high-value sources in our data.
- **Treating GEO tactics as originality:** tactics for _generative engine optimization_ (GEO) don’t add new evidence, and a 2026 critical survey found no GEO technique with a proven, durable effect on discoverability.
- **Inventing or “illustrating” statistics:** publish no number you can’t trace to a method, a sample, and a date.
- **Publishing unverified AI drafts:** check each claim at its source before publishing, as in step 7.
- **Burying the finding:** put your new data point on the first screen, ahead of background.

GEO covers tactics meant to raise a page’s share of AI answers. Olivier Martinez of Sciences Po reviewed 45 GEO studies in [a 2026 critical survey of GEO research](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.14035v1). He found “no technique with a stable, longitudinal, cross-platform causal effect on organic discoverability or downstream clicks and conversions.”

The survey rejects the popular 40% visibility lift figure as a general claim. It rates extractable, verifiable evidence (figures, definitions, and quotes) as moderate support. It’s a preprint by a single author and hasn’t gone through peer review, so we read it as a caution against shortcuts.

## FAQs about creating original content

### How do you make your content stand out from AI-generated and competitor content?

Lead with evidence other pages can’t copy, and name your sources in the sentence. [Google’s scaled content abuse policy](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies) targets “unoriginal content that provides little to no value to users, no matter how it’s created.” In [Graphite’s study of AI content in search](https://graphite.io/five-percent/research/ai-content-in-search-and-llms), Graphite found that humans wrote 86% of articles ranking in Google. The figure for articles cited by ChatGPT and Perplexity was 82%, in a June 2025 sample measured with AI detectors (observational).

### How do you perform SEO research before creating content?

Pick the query, review its search volume and related questions, and read the top pages and the sources AI answers cite. Note the intent and the format readers expect. Then record the gaps in a consensus map, as in step 4.

### Why is expert perspective important for creating authentic content?

Experts supply firsthand experience and judgment that summaries lack, and they can explain tradeoffs. Danny Sullivan, a director within Google Search, made a related point in August 2025. In [Danny Sullivan’s WordCamp keynote remarks](https://searchengineland.com/google-danny-sullivan-good-seo-good-geo-461464), he said, “Are you saying write about things that are unique or interesting? Cool. That’s good for people.” Interview your own engineers, customers, or practitioners, and quote them by name.

### How do you tailor content for different social media platforms?

Keep the original finding constant and change the packaging for each platform: native format, length, and hook. Lead with the data point in the first line or the first frame. Link back to the full source so readers can get the method and depth.
